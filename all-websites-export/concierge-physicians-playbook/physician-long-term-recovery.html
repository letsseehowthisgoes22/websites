The Role of the Concierge Physician in Long-Term Recovery

Continuity of Care · January 2026

The treatment industry focuses, understandably, on the acute phase of recovery: detoxification, residential treatment, intensive outpatient programming. But the evidence consistently demonstrates that sustained recovery is a long-term process — the National Institute on Drug Abuse characterizes addiction as a chronic, relapsing disorder with relapse rates of 40-60%, comparable to those for hypertension and type 2 diabetes. The concierge physician, uniquely positioned as a longitudinal presence in the patient's life, has a role in supporting recovery that extends far beyond the initial referral.

The Post-Treatment Transition

The period immediately following discharge from residential treatment is among the highest-risk periods for relapse. The patient returns to the environment — the home, the office, the social circles — in which their substance use developed. The structure imposed by a treatment program is replaced by the unstructured reality of daily life. And the support systems that sustained the patient during treatment (therapists, peers, clinical staff) are no longer immediately available.

The concierge physician should be an active participant in the discharge planning process. This means communicating with the treatment team prior to discharge (with the patient's consent under 42 CFR Part 2) to understand the recommended aftercare plan, the medications prescribed, the ongoing therapy schedule, and any concerns about the patient's readiness for discharge. It means scheduling a follow-up appointment within the first week of the patient's return — not for a specific medical complaint, but as a touchpoint that provides clinical continuity and early detection of any emerging problems.

The aftercare plan should include a coordinated team: an addiction psychiatrist or therapist providing ongoing behavioral health treatment, a recovery support professional (recovery coach or sober companion) providing daily structure and accountability during the early months, the concierge physician providing medical oversight and longitudinal monitoring, and the patient's family office or personal staff, informed to the extent appropriate about how to support the patient's recovery without enabling behavior.

Medical Monitoring in Recovery

The concierge physician's medical oversight in recovery should include several specific elements that go beyond routine primary care.

Laboratory Monitoring

Regular laboratory testing provides objective data about the patient's physiological status. For patients recovering from alcohol use disorder, liver function tests (AST, ALT, GGT) provide a longitudinal marker of liver health and, indirectly, of sobriety — GGT is particularly sensitive to recent alcohol consumption. Comprehensive metabolic panels, CBC with differential, and nutritional markers (B12, folate, vitamin D, magnesium) should be monitored regularly during the first year of recovery. For patients on medication-assisted treatment, drug screening panels should be conducted as clinically appropriate — not as surveillance, but as a clinical tool that provides data for treatment adjustment.

Medication Management

Many patients in recovery take medications that require monitoring by a primary care physician. As recent clinical commentary has noted, medication adherence technology continues to fail the patients who need it most — making the concierge physician's direct oversight all the more critical. Naltrexone (oral or extended-release injectable) requires liver function monitoring. Buprenorphine, prescribed for opioid use disorder, requires periodic assessment for diversion risk and therapeutic adequacy. Psychiatric medications prescribed for co-occurring conditions (antidepressants, mood stabilizers, anxiolytics) require monitoring for side effects and drug interactions. The concierge physician should coordinate medication management with the patient's addiction psychiatrist to avoid therapeutic conflicts and ensure integrated care.

Physical Health Restoration

Active substance use disorders frequently cause or exacerbate physical health conditions that require attention during recovery. Chronic alcohol use damages the liver, pancreas, cardiovascular system, and nervous system. Stimulant use affects cardiovascular and neurological health. Opioid use disorders are associated with infectious disease risk (hepatitis C, HIV) and hormonal dysregulation. The concierge physician should develop a comprehensive health restoration plan that addresses these substance-specific sequelae in the context of the patient's recovery.

Recognizing Relapse Indicators

The concierge physician's regular contact with the patient creates opportunities to identify early indicators of relapse that might not be apparent to others. These indicators are often subtle and medical: changes in sleep patterns reported during routine visits, weight fluctuations, elevated blood pressure or heart rate without explanation, requests for prescriptions of controlled substances (particularly benzodiazepines or sleep medications), missed appointments or rescheduled visits, and changes in laboratory values that suggest resumed substance use.

The "Wellness Visit" Framework: Some concierge physicians have formalized their role in recovery support by establishing a "recovery wellness visit" schedule — quarterly appointments specifically focused on the patient's recovery status, physical health markers, and coordination with the behavioral health team. This structured approach normalizes the physician's involvement in recovery and creates a regular checkpoint that might otherwise be lost in the flow of routine medical care.

Navigating the Recovery Support Ecosystem

The recovery support ecosystem for UHNW patients has expanded significantly in recent years. Beyond traditional therapy and twelve-step participation, patients now have access to professional recovery coaches, sober companions who provide 24/7 support during the early months of recovery, and comprehensive care management firms that coordinate all aspects of the patient's recovery. Firms in this space include O'Connor Professional Group, which provides care management for complex behavioral health situations in UHNW families, and specialized sober companion agencies that place credentialed professionals with clients for defined engagement periods. The professional standards and certification landscape for recovery support practitioners have evolved considerably in recent years.

The concierge physician can play a valuable role in evaluating these services. Not all recovery support providers are equally qualified, and the UHNW market attracts both exceptional practitioners and opportunistic ones. The physician should inquire about the recovery support professional's clinical credentials (Certified Addiction Recovery Coach, Certified Professional Recovery Coach, or equivalent), their experience with UHNW clients and the specific demands of that population, their communication protocols with the medical team, and their approach to maintaining professional boundaries.

The Family Dimension

In UHNW families, the physician often serves as the primary care provider for multiple family members. This creates both an opportunity and a challenge. The opportunity is a holistic view of the family system — the physician may observe the impact of the patient's recovery (or relapse) on other family members, the dynamics that support or undermine recovery, and the health of the family system as a whole. The challenge is maintaining appropriate boundaries: the physician's primary obligation is to the patient, and the patient's confidentiality must be respected even within a family that the physician serves collectively.

When family members seek information about the patient's recovery, the physician should redirect them to the patient's care manager or therapist rather than serving as an information conduit. When family members present with their own health concerns that may be related to the stress of the patient's condition, the physician should address those concerns independently while recognizing the interconnected nature of family health.

The Long View

Recovery is not a destination but a process. The concierge physician who commits to supporting a patient's recovery over years — through the initial fragility of early sobriety, through the inevitable challenges and setbacks, through the gradual rebuilding of health and function — provides a continuity of care that no other professional in the patient's life can match. This is among the most impactful services a concierge physician can provide, and one of the least recognized.

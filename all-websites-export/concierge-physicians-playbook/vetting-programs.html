Vetting Private Addiction Treatment Programs: A Physician's Guide

Clinical Vetting · January 2026

The luxury addiction treatment market has expanded dramatically over the past two decades. A physician referring a UHNW patient to residential treatment now faces a landscape of programs charging $30,000 to $130,000 per week — with a correlation between price and amenities that is far stronger than the correlation between price and clinical outcomes. The physician's obligation is to distinguish between programs that charge premium fees for premium clinical care and programs that charge premium fees primarily for premium real estate.

The Accreditation Baseline

Accreditation provides a minimum threshold for evaluation, not a ceiling. The two primary accrediting bodies in the United States — the Joint Commission and CARF International — evaluate programs against defined standards for clinical care, safety, and organizational management. Joint Commission accreditation is more common among hospital-based programs and larger facilities; CARF accreditation is more common in specialized addiction treatment settings.

In the UK, the Care Quality Commission (CQC) inspects and rates treatment facilities on five key dimensions: safety, effectiveness, caring, responsiveness, and whether the service is well-led. The Priory Group, the UK's largest independent provider of mental health services, operates under CQC oversight, as does Nightingale Hospital in central London. For Swiss programs, there is no single equivalent accrediting body, but cantonal health authorities license and inspect treatment facilities, and some Swiss programs — notably The Kusnacht Practice — have obtained Joint Commission International (JCI) accreditation.

Accreditation tells you that a program meets minimum standards. It does not tell you whether the program is clinically excellent, whether its approach is appropriate for your patient, or whether the program's actual practices match its marketing. For that, you need deeper evaluation.

Clinical Leadership

The single most important indicator of a treatment program's clinical quality is its clinical leadership. The physician evaluating a program should ask who serves as medical director and what their qualifications are. Board certification in addiction medicine (through the American Board of Preventive Medicine) or addiction psychiatry (through the American Board of Psychiatry and Neurology) should be considered the minimum standard for clinical leadership of a program treating substance use disorders. Programs whose medical directors lack addiction-specific board certification should be viewed with skepticism, regardless of their amenities.

The physician should also evaluate the depth of the clinical team. How many psychiatrists are on staff? What is the ratio of licensed clinicians to patients? Are therapists conducting individual therapy sessions, or is the therapeutic program primarily group-based? Programs with a high ratio of experiential activities (yoga, equine therapy, art therapy) to evidence-based individual therapy may be offering an appealing lifestyle experience rather than intensive clinical treatment.

Evidence-Based Modalities: The National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) identifies several therapeutic approaches with strong evidence bases for substance use disorders: cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), motivational interviewing/motivational enhancement therapy, contingency management, community reinforcement approach, and medication-assisted treatment (MAT). A program that does not employ at least several of these modalities as core components of its treatment protocol should prompt questions about its clinical orientation.

The Questions to Ask

When evaluating a luxury treatment program for a UHNW patient, the following questions yield the most useful information.

On Clinical Approach

What is the program's position on medication-assisted treatment for opioid use disorders? Programs that categorically refuse to prescribe or maintain buprenorphine or naltrexone are operating contrary to the weight of clinical evidence. What assessment tools does the program use for treatment planning? The ASAM Criteria is the gold standard. Programs that cannot articulate a structured assessment methodology should raise concerns. What is the average length of stay, and how is discharge readiness determined? Programs that discharge patients on a fixed schedule (28 days, 45 days) regardless of clinical progress may be prioritizing business operations over clinical needs.

On Staffing

What is the patient-to-therapist ratio for individual therapy? In premium programs, patients should receive a minimum of three to four hours of individual therapy per week with a licensed clinician. What is the staff turnover rate? High turnover among clinical staff is a red flag that may indicate management problems, underpayment, or a toxic clinical culture. Does the program employ addiction medicine physicians or rely on general practitioners for medical oversight?

On Outcomes

Does the program track post-discharge outcomes, and if so, what methodology does it use? Many programs cite completion rates (the percentage of patients who finish the program) rather than abstinence or recovery rates at six and twelve months post-discharge. Completion rates are a measure of retention, not efficacy. Is the program willing to share its outcomes data? Programs that make outcome claims in marketing materials but decline to provide supporting data should be treated with suspicion. Does the program participate in any external outcomes monitoring or research collaborations?

Red Flags

Certain features of a program's marketing or operations should prompt heightened scrutiny. Celebrity endorsements or testimonials, whether explicit or implied, suggest a marketing orientation that may not reflect clinical priorities. Guaranteed outcomes or success rate claims that are not supported by published, independently verified data. Resistance to providing detailed clinical information to the referring physician. An emphasis on amenities (private chefs, luxury vehicles, beachfront locations) that significantly outweighs the emphasis on clinical programming in marketing materials. A clinical program that is primarily group-based with limited individual therapy time.

It is worth noting that some programs have faced significant legal and regulatory challenges. Sierra Tucson in Arizona was subject to a $3 million jury verdict following a patient death and has been fined for deficiencies in psychiatric care. Passages Malibu has faced multiple lawsuits, including employment disputes and challenges over advertising practices. Promises Malibu, once among the most prominent celebrity treatment centers, closed in 2023 following its parent company's bankruptcy. These histories do not necessarily disqualify a program, but they warrant investigation.

Program Categories

The One-Client-at-a-Time Model

Paracelsus Recovery in Zurich pioneered the one-client-at-a-time treatment model, in which the entire clinical team is dedicated to a single patient. This model offers maximum privacy and truly individualized care, but at the highest cost point in the industry (approximately CHF 95,000 per week). The clinical advantage is that treatment is fully personalized rather than adapted from a group template. The disadvantage is the absence of peer interaction, which is a therapeutic element in group-based programs.

The Executive Program

Several programs offer executive tracks that accommodate ongoing business obligations — secure communication facilities, flexible scheduling that allows for business calls and meetings, and shorter or more flexible treatment durations. Caron Treatment Centers, Hazelden Betty Ford, and The Dunes East Hampton all offer executive-oriented programming. The clinical question is whether these accommodations support or undermine treatment — a patient who is managing a corporate crisis from within a treatment facility may not be fully engaged in the therapeutic process.

The In-Home Model

An emerging alternative to residential treatment is the in-home model, in which a clinical team delivers treatment in the patient's residence. ALYST Health, based in Los Angeles, is the first at-home addiction treatment program to receive Joint Commission accreditation. For a more detailed examination of in-home clinical models, The Private Client's analysis provides a thorough framework. This model may be appropriate for patients who cannot leave their responsibilities for an extended period, but it requires careful evaluation of the clinical infrastructure — a sober companion and a therapist visiting twice a week does not constitute a treatment program. The in-home model works when it replicates the intensity of residential treatment in a home setting; it fails when it serves primarily as a mechanism for the patient to avoid the discomfort of leaving their environment.

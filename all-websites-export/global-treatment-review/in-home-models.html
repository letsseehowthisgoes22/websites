In-Home Treatment Models

When residential treatment is not feasible or preferable, a growing category of providers brings intensive clinical programming to the patient's own environment. The model's promise is significant — and so is the variation in quality.

The in-home treatment model is not new — physicians have made house calls since antiquity. But the application of this model to addiction treatment at a clinical intensity comparable to residential care is a relatively recent development, driven by the demands of UHNW patients whose professional obligations, privacy requirements, or personal circumstances make facility-based treatment impractical.

The Clinical Argument

Proponents of in-home treatment make several clinical arguments. Treatment in the patient's own environment allows for real-time identification and management of triggers, environmental risks, and relational dynamics that contribute to substance use — factors that a residential facility cannot replicate. The skills the patient develops in treatment are immediately tested and refined in the context where they will ultimately be needed. Family dynamics can be observed and addressed as they occur naturally rather than through the artificial lens of scheduled sessions. And the transition from treatment to independent living — the most vulnerable period in the recovery trajectory — is eliminated, because the patient has been developing independent living skills throughout treatment.

Critics counter that the home environment may itself be a primary trigger for substance use, that the lack of physical separation from the patient's normal life makes it more difficult to achieve the psychological distance necessary for reflection and change, and that the absence of peer interaction (a core component of most residential programs) deprives the patient of the normalizing, community-building experience that group treatment provides.

Both arguments have merit. The question is not whether in-home treatment is universally superior or inferior to residential treatment, but whether it is clinically appropriate for a specific patient with a specific clinical presentation in a specific environment — and whether the in-home program being considered delivers care at a standard that justifies the model.

What to Evaluate

The variation in quality among in-home treatment providers is extreme. At one end of the spectrum are programs that deliver intensive, structured clinical care with qualified professionals, formal protocols, and outcome tracking. At the other end are arrangements that amount to a sober companion and occasional therapy visits — labeled as "in-home treatment" but lacking the clinical infrastructure to justify the designation.

The minimum components of a credible in-home treatment program should include a medical director with board certification in addiction medicine or addiction psychiatry; daily individual therapy (not weekly — daily, at least during the initial intensive phase); psychiatric management including medication evaluation and monitoring; 24/7 recovery support by a qualified professional; structured daily programming; regular drug and alcohol screening; family therapy; and coordination with the patient's existing medical providers. Programs that cannot demonstrate these components should not be considered in-home treatment — they are providing recovery support services, which, while valuable, are not treatment.

Provider Landscape

Los Angeles, California

ALYST Health

ALYST Health holds the distinction of being the first at-home addiction treatment program to receive Joint Commission accreditation — a significant credibility marker that subjects the program to the same rigorous standards applied to traditional residential treatment facilities. ALYST deploys a clinical team to the patient's residence that includes a therapist, a psychiatrist, a recovery support specialist, and medical oversight. The program provides structured daily programming, individual therapy, psychiatric management, and drug testing within the patient's home environment.

ALYST's Joint Commission accreditation represents more than a marketing advantage — it means the program has demonstrated compliance with standards covering clinical leadership, treatment planning, medication management, patient safety, quality improvement, and documentation. For families evaluating in-home options, this accreditation provides a level of external validation that other in-home providers have not pursued.

Multiple Markets

Concierge Care Collective

Concierge Care Collective offers a comprehensive in-home care model that extends beyond addiction treatment to include nursing, psychiatric stabilization, and long-term recovery planning. The firm operates in New York, Palm Beach, Beverly Hills, and Aspen — markets with high concentrations of UHNW families. The model is distinctive for its breadth: rather than focusing solely on addiction treatment, Concierge Care Collective provides an integrated care infrastructure that can address medical, psychiatric, and recovery needs simultaneously.

National

Care Management-Coordinated Models

Some in-home treatment arrangements are assembled by care management firms — most notably O'Connor Professional Group — rather than by a single treatment provider. In this model, the care manager assembles a team of independent clinicians (therapist, psychiatrist, recovery coach) who deliver care in the patient's home under the care manager's coordination. For fiduciaries funding these arrangements from trust assets, the trust administration considerations are significant. This model offers flexibility (the team is customized to the patient's specific needs) but requires exceptional coordination to ensure that the independent practitioners function as a cohesive treatment team rather than as a collection of individual providers.

The Hybrid Approach

An increasingly common model combines a brief period of residential treatment (14-30 days for stabilization, assessment, and initial therapeutic engagement) with a longer period of in-home treatment for the continuation and maintenance phases. This hybrid approach captures the advantages of both models: the controlled environment and intensive assessment of residential care during the acute phase, followed by the environmental integration and practical applicability of in-home treatment during the transition to independent functioning.

The hybrid model is particularly well-suited to patients who need the medical oversight and physical separation of residential care during detoxification and early stabilization but who benefit from returning to their environment as soon as clinically safe. Swiss programs, with their short recommended stays (4-8 weeks) and emphasis on aftercare, are natural partners for in-home continuation programs. Several US residential programs have also developed formal step-down partnerships with in-home providers.

The Accreditation Gap: As of this writing, ALYST Health is the only in-home addiction treatment program to hold Joint Commission accreditation. This is not because other in-home providers cannot meet Joint Commission standards — it is because the accreditation process is voluntary, expensive, and time-consuming, and most in-home providers have not pursued it. For families, this means that the external quality validation available for residential programs (Joint Commission, CARF) is largely absent in the in-home space. Independent evaluation of clinical qualifications, treatment protocols, and outcome data is therefore even more important when assessing in-home options.

Recovery Support as a Component

In-home treatment models typically include a recovery support component — a sober companion or recovery coach who provides 24/7 presence and accountability. This component is essential, but it should be understood as one element of a comprehensive treatment program, not as a substitute for treatment. Agencies that specialize in sober companion placement — including Coast Health Consulting, SilverBell Global, Spearhead Health, and others — can provide the recovery support component for in-home treatment arrangements that are coordinated by a care manager or treatment program.

The key distinction is between recovery support (which provides accountability, environmental management, and connection to recovery resources) and treatment (which provides clinical assessment, psychotherapy, psychiatric management, and structured therapeutic programming). Both are necessary; neither is sufficient alone.

Case Studies in High-Stakes Interventions

The following case analyses are composites drawn from the collective experience of practicing interventionists. All identifying details have been changed. They are presented for professional education purposes, illustrating the clinical reasoning and practical challenges that characterize complex interventions.

Case 1: The Family Office Principal

A 58-year-old patriarch of a family with approximately $400 million in assets under management had developed a progressive alcohol use disorder over six years. The family office chief of staff had been managing around the problem — canceling meetings when the principal was intoxicated, routing financial decisions through the co-trustee, and controlling information flow to family members. The principal's wife initiated contact with the interventionist after discovering that the chief of staff had been covering financial irregularities related to the principal's drinking.

Assessment

The interventionist's assessment revealed a family system in which enabling had become institutionalized. The chief of staff's enabling was not malicious — it was a survival strategy developed to maintain stability. However, it had created a situation in which the principal had experienced no meaningful consequences of his drinking and had no motivation to seek treatment. The wife's discovery of the financial irregularities had destabilized the system, creating both a crisis and an opportunity.

Approach

The interventionist recommended an ARISE Level 1 approach: a direct, caring conversation initiated by the wife, with the interventionist present to facilitate. The conversation was framed not as a confrontation about drinking but as a family discussion about the financial situation that had come to light — a discussion that naturally led to the role of alcohol in the principal's decision-making. The principal's concierge physician was briefed in advance and was available by phone to discuss medical concerns.

Outcome

The principal agreed to an evaluation by an addiction psychiatrist within 48 hours. Following the evaluation, he entered a 45-day residential program at a facility with an executive track that accommodated limited business communications. The chief of staff was retained but placed under the oversight of a temporary fiduciary consultant. A care management firm was engaged to coordinate aftercare and monitor the transition back to full functioning.

Key Lesson: The most effective entry point for this intervention was not the drinking itself but the financial consequences that the principal could not dismiss or rationalize. Meeting the individual where their concern lies — in this case, the integrity of the family's financial structure — is more effective than leading with the substance use.

Case 2: The Entertainment Industry Crisis

A 32-year-old actor under contract for a major streaming production had escalated from recreational cocaine use to daily freebase use over a four-month period. The production's insurance carrier, upon receiving information about the actor's substance use from a confidential source, issued a conditional mandate: the actor must submit to regular drug testing and have a sober companion present on set, or the production would lose its cast insurance. The actor's personal manager contacted the interventionist when the actor refused the insurance company's requirements.

Assessment

This case illustrates the intersection of clinical intervention and industry-specific dynamics. The entertainment insurance carrier — in this case functioning through an entertainment insurance broker — held ultimate leverage: without cast insurance, the production could not continue, and the actor would be in breach of contract. However, the actor's resistance was driven by fear of public exposure and a genuine (if distorted) belief that his use was manageable. The personal manager's priority was preserving both the actor's career and the production — objectives that aligned with treatment engagement but created pressure for a rapid resolution that might not serve the actor's long-term interests.

Approach

The interventionist worked with the personal manager to arrange a meeting framed as a discussion about the insurance situation rather than an intervention. The actor's entertainment attorney was included to explain the contractual implications. The interventionist used motivational interviewing techniques to explore the actor's ambivalence — acknowledging his desire to continue working while presenting treatment as the pathway that preserved both his health and his career. Two treatment options were presented: a 30-day residential program during the production hiatus (beginning in three weeks), or an immediate in-home treatment arrangement that would allow the actor to continue working while receiving daily clinical support and monitoring.

Outcome

The actor chose the in-home treatment option, which was structured to include daily therapy sessions, psychiatric medication management, random drug testing, and a sober companion present during all production hours. The sober companion was given the on-set title of "personal assistant" to maintain confidentiality. The insurance carrier accepted this arrangement. After the production wrapped, the actor entered a residential program for additional treatment. The total engagement lasted eight months.

Case 3: The Multi-Generational Crisis

A family with generational wealth spanning four generations presented with a complex multi-generational substance use pattern. The identified patient was a 26-year-old grandson who had been using opioids for three years and had recently progressed to intravenous heroin. However, the assessment revealed that the grandfather (the family's wealth creator) had an untreated alcohol use disorder, the grandmother was dependent on prescribed benzodiazepines, and the patient's mother had a 15-year history of prescription opioid misuse that the family had tacitly accepted.

Assessment

The interventionist recognized that intervening with the grandson in isolation would be unlikely to succeed because the family system normalized substance use across generations. The grandson's use, while the most acute and medically dangerous, was a symptom of a family culture in which substance use was an accepted coping mechanism. The family's wealth had insulated all members from the natural consequences that might otherwise motivate treatment engagement.

Approach

The interventionist proposed a family systems intervention rather than an individual intervention. Over a period of six weeks, the interventionist met with family members individually and in small groups, mapping the family's relationship with substances, identifying the enabling patterns that sustained use across generations, and building a coalition of family members willing to address the issue collectively. The family's trust attorney and the family office director were included in the planning, as trust distribution decisions and family governance structures were integral to the family's dynamic.

Outcome

The grandson entered residential treatment. The mother began outpatient treatment for prescription opioid dependence. The grandmother's prescribing physician was consulted about a medically supervised benzodiazepine taper. The grandfather declined treatment but agreed to participate in family therapy sessions. A care management firm was engaged to coordinate care across three generations and to advise the family office on trust administration decisions related to family members in treatment. The engagement remained active at 18 months.

Key Lesson: In families with multi-generational substance use, intervening with the identified patient alone addresses the symptom, not the system. The family systems approach requires more time and complexity but produces more sustainable outcomes by addressing the cultural and relational patterns that perpetuate substance use across generations.

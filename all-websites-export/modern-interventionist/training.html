Training and Certification Programs

The intervention field lacks a single, universally recognized credentialing pathway — a reality that creates both opportunities and challenges for practitioners seeking to establish professional credibility. The following overview maps the primary certification options and training programs available to aspiring and practicing interventionists.

The Certification Landscape

Unlike fields such as medicine, law, or even addiction counseling, intervention practice does not require a specific license or certification in most jurisdictions. This regulatory gap means that anyone can call themselves an interventionist — a reality that has attracted both dedicated professionals and opportunistic practitioners to the field. For the serious practitioner, voluntary certification serves as a signal of competence, ethical commitment, and professional seriousness.

Primary Certification Pathways

Certified Intervention Professional (CIP)

Offered by the Association of Intervention Specialists Certification Board (AISCB), the CIP is the most widely recognized certification in the intervention field. Requirements include documentation of a minimum number of supervised interventions, completion of approved training hours, adherence to the AISCB Code of Ethics, passing the CIP certification examination, and ongoing continuing education to maintain certification. The CIP credential is recognized by treatment programs, referral sources, and families as indicating a baseline level of competence and professional commitment.

Board Registered Interventionist (BRI)

The BRI credential, offered through the Pennsylvania Certification Board (PCB) and recognized in several states, provides another credentialing pathway with specific educational and experiential requirements. The BRI is particularly relevant for interventionists practicing in states that have adopted or recognize PCB credentials.

ARISE Certification

Practitioners who specialize in the ARISE intervention model can obtain certification through the ARISE Network's training program. This certification is model-specific — it certifies competence in the ARISE methodology rather than intervention practice generally — but it carries significant weight because of the ARISE model's strong evidence base. The training includes didactic instruction, supervised practice, and ongoing case consultation.

Foundational Training Programs

Love First Intervention Training

Based on the work of Jeff Jay and Debra Jay, the Love First training program teaches a structured, family-centered intervention approach that emphasizes preparation, compassion, and dignity. The program is offered as multi-day intensive workshops and includes ongoing mentorship opportunities.

CRAFT Training

While CRAFT (Community Reinforcement and Family Training) is typically taught to family members, clinicians and interventionists who complete CRAFT training gain a powerful set of skills for working with families. Multiple academic centers offer CRAFT training, including the University of New Mexico's Center on Alcoholism, Substance Abuse, and Addictions (CASAA), where the model was developed.

Motivational Interviewing Training

Motivational interviewing (MI) is a foundational skill for modern intervention practice. The Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers (MINT) maintains a directory of certified trainers who offer MI workshops ranging from introductory to advanced levels. MI training is increasingly considered essential for any intervention professional, regardless of their primary intervention model.

Clinical Background Pathways

Many successful interventionists enter the field from clinical backgrounds that provide relevant training and credentials. Licensed Clinical Social Workers (LCSW) bring assessment skills, family systems expertise, and clinical supervision capability. Licensed Professional Counselors (LPC) and Licensed Marriage and Family Therapists (LMFT) bring therapeutic skills and understanding of relational dynamics. Certified Addiction Counselors (CAC/CADC) bring specialized knowledge of substance use disorders and the treatment continuum. Physicians with addiction medicine training bring clinical authority and the ability to assess medical acuity.

These clinical licenses, combined with intervention-specific training and certification, create the most credible professional profile in the field. The interventionist who holds both a clinical license and an intervention certification demonstrates both the clinical foundation and the specialized expertise that families and referral sources seek.

Continuing Education

The intervention field evolves rapidly, and practitioners must maintain current knowledge through ongoing education. Key continuing education opportunities include the annual conferences of the Association of Intervention Specialists, the National Conference on Addiction Disorders (NCAD), ARISE Network continuing education workshops, publications including the Journal of Substance Abuse Treatment and the Journal of Marital and Family Therapy, and peer consultation groups — structured case review meetings with fellow interventionists that provide both education and professional support.

The Professionalization Imperative: The intervention field is at a critical juncture. As the practice becomes more sophisticated and the stakes (particularly in UHNW families) become higher, the demand for qualified, credentialed professionals will increase. Practitioners who invest in training, certification, and ongoing professional development now are positioning themselves for the future of the field — a future in which families, treatment programs, and referral sources will increasingly expect demonstrated competence rather than accepting self-reported experience.

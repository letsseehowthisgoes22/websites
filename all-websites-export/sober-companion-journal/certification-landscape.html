The Certification Landscape: CARC, CPRC, and Beyond

Certification · January 2026

The recovery support profession lacks a single, universally recognized credentialing pathway — a reality that reflects the field's organic development and its position at the intersection of peer support, clinical paraprofessional practice, and personal service. Multiple organizations offer certifications with varying requirements, recognition, and practical value. For practitioners navigating this landscape, and for agencies and families evaluating practitioners' qualifications, a clear-eyed assessment of available certifications is essential.

National Certifications

Certified Addiction Recovery Coach (CARC)

Offered by the Connecticut Community for Addiction Recovery (CCAR), the CARC is one of the most recognized credentials in the recovery coaching field. CCAR's Recovery Coach Academy provides foundational training in recovery coaching principles, ethics, and practice. The CARC certification requires completion of the Recovery Coach Academy (approximately 30 hours), documented experience providing recovery support, adherence to CCAR's ethical standards, and ongoing continuing education. The CARC is well-respected in the recovery community and provides a solid foundation in peer-based recovery support principles. Its limitation is that it is primarily oriented toward community-based recovery coaching rather than the intensive, 24/7 sober companion role — practitioners in the UHNW space typically need additional training beyond what the CARC provides.

Certified Professional Recovery Coach (CPRC)

The International Association of Professional Recovery Coaches (IAPRC) offers the CPRC credential, which is specifically designed for practitioners providing professional recovery coaching services. The CPRC requires a more extensive training curriculum than many peer-based certifications, including training in motivational interviewing, relapse prevention planning, ethics and boundary management, and business practices for recovery coaching. The CPRC is increasingly recognized in the professional recovery support market and is particularly relevant for practitioners serving private-pay clients.

Recovery Coach Professional (RCP)

The RCP credential, recognized in several states, provides another pathway for recovery support practitioners. Requirements vary by state but typically include a combination of education, supervised experience, and examination. The RCP is particularly relevant in states that have developed formal recovery support specialist roles within their behavioral health systems.

State-Level Certifications

Many states have developed their own recovery support specialist or peer support specialist certifications, often as part of Medicaid-funded recovery support programs. These certifications vary significantly in their requirements, but they typically include training in recovery support principles, ethics, and cultural competency. State certifications are important because they are increasingly required for reimbursement under Medicaid and other payer systems, and because they represent the state's recognition of recovery support as a legitimate professional role.

Notable state programs include Pennsylvania's Certified Recovery Specialist (CRS) credential, administered by the Pennsylvania Certification Board; Ohio's Certified Peer Recovery Supporter; and Florida's Certified Recovery Peer Specialist. These state-level credentials are primarily oriented toward community-based practice and may not fully address the scope of practice required in UHNW sober companion work, but they provide a regulatory foundation that the field as a whole benefits from.

Complementary Credentials

Many practitioners in the sober companion field hold credentials beyond the recovery-specific certifications described above. These complementary credentials can significantly enhance a practitioner's clinical capability and professional credibility.

Licensed clinical credentials — LCSW, LPC, LMFT, CADC — provide training in assessment, therapeutic techniques, and clinical ethics that is directly applicable to advanced recovery support practice. Practitioners who hold both a clinical license and a recovery support credential represent the highest tier of qualification in the field. Certified intervention professional (CIP) credentials, described elsewhere, are relevant for practitioners whose scope includes family engagement and crisis management. Specialized training in motivational interviewing (MI), demonstrated through MINT certification or documented MI training hours, is particularly valuable. Training in trauma-informed care, recognizing that a high percentage of individuals with substance use disorders have histories of trauma, is increasingly considered essential.

The Credential Gap: There is currently no certification specifically designed for the UHNW sober companion role — a role that requires not only recovery support competencies but also the ability to operate in high-net-worth environments, manage complex confidentiality requirements, coordinate with multi-disciplinary care teams, and maintain professional boundaries under the unique pressures of 24/7 engagement with clients of significant wealth and social position. The development of such a credential — potentially through collaboration between existing certification bodies and agencies that specialize in UHNW recovery support — would address a significant gap in the profession's credentialing infrastructure.

Evaluating Certifications

For practitioners choosing a certification pathway, and for agencies and families evaluating practitioners' credentials, several factors should guide assessment. What are the training requirements — how many hours, what topics, and what is the quality of the training curriculum? What are the experience requirements — does the certification require documented practice experience, and is that experience supervised? What is the examination process — does the certification require a knowledge-based examination, and if so, what does the examination cover? What are the ethical standards — does the certifying body maintain a code of ethics and a disciplinary process? What is the continuing education requirement — does the certification require ongoing professional development? And what is the recognition of the credential — is the certification recognized by employers, payers, and referral sources in the practitioner's market?

No single certification is sufficient preparation for the complex demands of UHNW sober companion practice. The most qualified practitioners combine a foundation of recovery support certification with complementary clinical credentials, specialized training, and extensive supervised experience. The field's long-term credibility depends on practitioners who pursue this comprehensive preparation rather than relying on minimum credentials.

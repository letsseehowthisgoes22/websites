Clinical Frameworks for Recovery Support Practice

Clinical Frameworks · January 2026

The sober companion who operates on intuition and personal recovery experience alone is working without a map. While experiential knowledge is valuable — the practitioner who has navigated their own recovery brings an authenticity and empathy that cannot be taught — it is insufficient as a sole foundation for professional practice. Clinical frameworks provide the structure, language, and evidence base that distinguish professional recovery support from well-intentioned companionship.

Motivational Interviewing

Motivational interviewing (MI), developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick, is arguably the most important clinical framework for recovery support practitioners. MI is a collaborative, person-centered approach to communication that strengthens an individual's motivation for and commitment to change. It is not a technique — it is a way of being with a client that honors their autonomy while guiding them toward healthier choices.

The four processes of MI — engaging, focusing, evoking, and planning — provide a structure for virtually every interaction the sober companion has with the client. Engaging establishes the working relationship through empathic listening and genuine curiosity. Focusing identifies the direction of the conversation — what specific aspect of recovery is the current concern? Evoking draws out the client's own motivations for change — their reasons, desires, and abilities — rather than imposing external motivation. Planning develops concrete steps when the client is ready to act.

For the sober companion, MI skills are particularly relevant in moments of ambivalence — when the client is considering using, questioning the value of recovery, or resisting engagement with their treatment plan. The MI-trained companion can explore this ambivalence without judgment, elicit the client's own arguments for recovery, and support the client's autonomous decision-making. This is fundamentally different from the confrontational approach ("you can't use — think about what you'll lose") that is both less effective and more likely to damage the therapeutic relationship.

Research published in the Annual Review of Clinical Psychology (2005) and multiple meta-analyses have demonstrated MI's efficacy across substance use disorders (see also evidence-based treatment modalities), with effect sizes that are comparable to or greater than more intensive therapeutic interventions. For recovery support practitioners, MI training is available through the Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers (MINT) and through numerous continuing education providers.

Relapse Prevention

The relapse prevention (RP) model, developed by G. Alan Marlatt and refined by subsequent researchers, provides a cognitive-behavioral framework for understanding and managing relapse risk. The model identifies high-risk situations — interpersonal conflict, negative emotional states, social pressure, and environmental cues associated with previous use — and teaches coping strategies for navigating these situations without resorting to substance use.

For the sober companion, the RP model provides a practical framework for daily practice. It informs the companion's observation of the client's behavior and environment (identifying high-risk situations before they escalate), the companion's response to warning signs (implementing coping strategies collaboratively rather than reactively), the companion's communication with the clinical team (using RP language to describe observations and concerns), and the companion's approach to actual relapse events (understanding relapse as a process that begins with cognitive and behavioral precursors, not as a sudden event).

Marlatt's distinction between a "lapse" (a single episode of substance use) and a "relapse" (a return to the pre-treatment pattern of use) is particularly important for the sober companion. A lapse does not necessarily signal failure — the companion's response to a lapse can determine whether it remains an isolated event or escalates to a full relapse. The companion who responds to a lapse with clinical composure, immediate communication with the treatment team, and collaborative problem-solving provides far more value than one who responds with alarm, judgment, or panic.

The Recovery Capital Framework

Recovery capital, a concept developed by William White and colleagues, refers to the sum of resources — internal and external — that an individual can bring to bear on the initiation and maintenance of recovery. The framework identifies four categories of recovery capital: social capital (supportive relationships, recovery community involvement, family support), physical capital (financial resources, safe housing, transportation, health insurance), human capital (education, skills, health, self-efficacy, problem-solving abilities), and cultural capital (values, beliefs, and cultural practices that support recovery).

The recovery capital framework is particularly relevant for the sober companion working with UHNW clients, where the distribution of capital is unusual. Physical and financial capital are abundant — the client has unlimited resources, comfortable housing, and access to the best healthcare. But social capital may be impoverished — the client's social network may be heavily oriented toward substance use, and genuine supportive relationships may be scarce. Cultural capital may actively undermine recovery — the culture of wealth and privilege may normalize substance use and discourage the vulnerability required for recovery.

The Recovery Capital Paradox in UHNW Practice: Wealth simultaneously provides extraordinary resources for recovery (access to the best treatment, ability to take time away from responsibilities, resources for comprehensive aftercare) and extraordinary obstacles (unlimited access to substances, social environments that normalize use, employees who enable, and the ability to avoid consequences). The sober companion's role can be understood as helping the client leverage their abundant physical capital while building the social, human, and cultural capital that sustains long-term recovery.

Strengths-Based Approach

The strengths-based approach, rooted in positive psychology and social work practice, focuses on identifying and building upon the client's existing strengths rather than cataloging deficits. For the sober companion, this means recognizing and reinforcing the client's healthy coping strategies, positive relationships, meaningful activities, and personal qualities that support recovery.

This approach is particularly important in the UHNW context because the client's identity is often heavily invested in competence, achievement, and self-sufficiency. A deficit-focused approach — emphasizing what the client cannot do, where they are failing, what they lack — can trigger shame and resistance. A strengths-based approach that acknowledges the client's capabilities while honestly addressing the areas where support is needed is more likely to maintain engagement and build the self-efficacy that sustains recovery.

Trauma-Informed Practice

The connection between trauma and substance use disorders is well established. The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) study, originally published by Felitti et al. in the American Journal of Preventive Medicine (1998), demonstrated a dose-response relationship between childhood adversity and adult substance use. Subsequent research has confirmed that a high percentage of individuals with substance use disorders have histories of trauma — and that effective treatment must address this underlying trauma rather than focusing solely on the substance use.

For the sober companion, trauma-informed practice means understanding that the client's behavior — including resistance, emotional reactivity, difficulty trusting, and relapse — may be driven by trauma responses rather than willful non-compliance. It means creating a recovery support environment that prioritizes safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration, and empowerment — the five principles of trauma-informed care identified by SAMHSA. It means avoiding re-traumatization through practices that are controlling, confrontational, or that replicate power dynamics from the client's trauma history. And it means recognizing the limits of the sober companion's role in trauma treatment — the companion supports the client's engagement with trauma-focused therapy; the companion does not provide trauma therapy.

Integration in Practice

These frameworks are not competing approaches — they are complementary lenses that, together, provide a comprehensive foundation for professional practice. The MI-trained companion communicates with the client in a way that supports autonomy and motivation. The RP-informed companion identifies and responds to relapse risk with clinical precision. The recovery capital framework guides the companion's assessment of the client's resources and needs. The strengths-based approach maintains the client's dignity and engagement. And trauma-informed practice ensures that the companion's approach does not inadvertently cause harm.

The practitioner who integrates these frameworks operates at a fundamentally different level than one who relies solely on personal experience. This integration is not automatic — it requires training, supervision, and ongoing professional development. But it is the standard toward which the profession should aspire.

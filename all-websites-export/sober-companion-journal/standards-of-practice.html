Defining Professional Standards in Sober Companionship

Standards of Practice · January 2026

The sober companion field occupies an unusual position in the behavioral health landscape. It is simultaneously essential — filling a critical gap in the continuum of care between residential treatment and independent living — and unregulated, lacking the licensure requirements, standardized training, and professional oversight structures that characterize other healthcare professions. This absence of formal regulation has allowed the field to grow rapidly in response to demand, but it has also created significant quality variability that undermines the profession's credibility and, more importantly, compromises client outcomes.

What follows is a proposed framework for professional standards — not a regulatory mandate, but a set of principles that the profession might adopt to distinguish qualified practitioners from the growing number of individuals who enter the field without adequate preparation.

The Current State of Practice

The sober companion role emerged organically from the intersection of twelve-step tradition (the concept of sponsorship and peer support) and the treatment industry's recognition that the post-discharge period represents a critical vulnerability. In its earliest iterations, the role was informal: a person in recovery who accompanied a newly discharged individual through the first days and weeks of independent living, providing support, accountability, and the reassurance of shared experience.

As the role became professionalized — particularly in the UHNW market, where families were willing to pay significant fees for 24/7 recovery support — the scope expanded beyond its peer-support origins. Today, sober companions are expected to function as clinical paraprofessionals: monitoring for signs of relapse, coordinating with treatment teams, managing medications, navigating complex family dynamics, and operating in environments (private aircraft, international travel, red-carpet events) that are far removed from the treatment center group room. This expanded clinical role mirrors the broader trend toward clinically integrated models across the behavioral health continuum, where even logistical transitions are recognized as therapeutic opportunities.

Yet the profession's regulatory framework has not kept pace with this expanded scope. In most jurisdictions, no license or certification is required to practice as a sober companion. The barrier to entry is, effectively, a personal history of recovery and the willingness to be available 24/7. While many practitioners bring substantial clinical training, professional credentials, and years of experience, others bring primarily their own recovery narrative — which, while valuable, is insufficient as a sole qualification for the clinical demands of the role.

Proposed Standards Framework

Minimum Qualifications

A credible standard for sober companion practice should require, at minimum, a recognized certification in recovery support (Certified Addiction Recovery Coach through Connecticut Community for Addiction Recovery, Certified Professional Recovery Coach, Recovery Coach Professional credential, or equivalent state-recognized credential); documented personal recovery of at least two years of continuous sobriety (the specific duration is debatable, but the principle that the practitioner should have established stability in their own recovery before assuming responsibility for another's is not); completion of training in crisis intervention, including suicide risk assessment and de-escalation; current CPR and first aid certification; a clear background check; and documented training in professional ethics, boundary management, and confidentiality requirements including 42 CFR Part 2 and HIPAA.

Scope of Practice

Perhaps the most critical standard is a clear definition of scope. The sober companion is not a therapist, counselor, or physician. They do not diagnose, treat, or prescribe. Their role is to provide recovery support within defined boundaries: maintaining a safe, substance-free environment; monitoring for behavioral indicators of relapse risk and communicating observations to the clinical team; facilitating the client's engagement with their recovery plan (therapy appointments, medication compliance, recovery meetings); providing reality-based feedback and accountability; supporting the client in developing independent recovery skills and routines; and communicating regularly with the broader care team (therapist, psychiatrist, care manager, family).

The sober companion who exceeds this scope — providing therapy, making clinical recommendations, adjusting medications, or substituting their judgment for that of the clinical team — is practicing outside their competence and potentially causing harm. A clear scope of practice protects both the client and the practitioner.

The Boundary Challenge: In 24/7 practice, the line between professional and personal relationship is under constant pressure. The sober companion eats meals with the client, travels with the client, and is present during the most vulnerable moments of the client's day. This intimacy creates a dynamic that requires extraordinary boundary discipline. The professional standard should include explicit training in boundary management, regular clinical supervision, and organizational structures that support the practitioner's ability to maintain appropriate professional distance.

Clinical Supervision

Sober companions, regardless of their experience level, should practice under some form of clinical supervision. This does not necessarily mean traditional clinical supervision as practiced in the counseling professions — it means a structured relationship with a licensed clinician who reviews cases, provides guidance on clinical boundaries, and serves as an escalation point for situations that exceed the sober companion's scope of practice. Agencies that employ sober companions should provide this supervision as a structural requirement; independent practitioners should establish supervision relationships independently.

Continuing Education

The recovery support field is evolving rapidly, and practitioners must maintain current knowledge. Cross-disciplinary publications — including clinical commentary on diagnostic language, medication adherence, and AI in behavioral health — increasingly inform the continuing education landscape. A professional standard should require a minimum number of continuing education hours annually, covering topics including substance use disorder neuroscience, pharmacotherapy updates (particularly medication-assisted treatment), co-occurring mental health conditions, cultural competency, ethics and boundary management, and specialized populations (adolescents, elderly individuals, UHNW clients, professionals in regulated industries).

The Professionalization Imperative

The sober companion profession faces a choice. It can continue to operate without uniform standards, accepting the quality variability and credibility challenges that accompany unregulated practice. Or it can move toward professionalization — developing and enforcing standards that raise the floor of practice quality, establish public trust, and create a pathway for the field's most talented practitioners to build sustainable, respected careers.

The incentives for professionalization are strong. Families and care managers are increasingly demanding credentials and verifiable qualifications when selecting recovery support providers. Insurance companies, as they begin to explore coverage for recovery support services, will require credentialing standards. Treatment programs prefer to refer to recovery support providers who meet defined professional standards. And practitioners themselves benefit from the structure, support, and professional identity that a credentialed profession provides.

The path forward likely involves collaboration among existing certification bodies, professional associations, treatment providers, and practitioners themselves to develop consensus standards that reflect the field's current reality while establishing aspirational benchmarks for its future. This journal is committed to contributing to that conversation.
